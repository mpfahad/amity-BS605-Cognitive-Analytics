Introduction
Experimental research is carried out using a systematic manner, where one set 
of variables is kept constant while the other set of variables is measured as the focus 
of the experiment. A highly effective quantitative research technique, experimental 
research is particularly useful when the researcher does not have enough information 
to arrive at meaningful conclusions and have to carry out experiments to prove facts.
3.1: Design of Experiments
Experimental research, thus, helps a researcher make important decisions. It 
is considered successful only when the researcher ensures that a change in the 
dependent variable is wholly due to a change in the independent variable

3.1.6: Designing Engineering Experiments
Experiments are an inherent component of the decision-making process in 
engineering. For instance, a civil engineer is examining the impact of various curing 
techniques on the average compressive strength of concrete. The experiment involves 
conducting tests on many concrete specimens using each recommended technique 
of curing. The compressive strength of each specimen is then measured. The data 
obtained from this experiment is utilised to ascertain the optimal curing process that 
yields the highest average compressive strength.
If there are just two curing procedures of interest, this experiment may be designed 
and analysed using statistical hypothesis methods for two samples. The researcher is 
focused on a certain variable, which is the procedures used for curing. There are only 
two options for this variable. If the researcher wants to establish which curing process 
yields the highest compressive strength, they may select the number of specimens to 
test using the operating characteristic curves. The t-test can then be used to evaluate if 
the means of the two methods vary. 
Several single-factor studies need the consideration of more than two levels of the 
factor.
For instance, the civil engineer may choose to examine five distinct curing 
techniques.
Statistically based experimental design techniques are useful in the engineering

employed to get the intended results. This model is said to be a seldom effective 
framework for evaluating the effects of a cause on an outcome.

characteristics of the participants. For illustrative purposes, in relation to their degrees

give essential information that acts as a foundation for taking appropriate actions.

based on the information shown by the chart. Control charts are versatile instruments

axes.
Control charts are divided into two broad categories based on the two types of data 
they handle: variable data and attribute data.

3.2.7: Don’t Know” Responses
The intention behind incorporating “don’t know” options in surveys is to provide 
respondents with an opportunity to abstain from questions they’re uncertain about. 
However, this approach may backfire as respondents might opt for these choices 
to avoid engaging with the question or topic entirely. Consequently, the reliability of 
survey responses could be compromised. To mitigate this issue, it’s crucial to explore 
alternative strategies, such as removing the option entirely or introducing follow-up 
questions to glean more insightful data.
Benefits of Offering an “Other” Option
Providing an “other” response alternative empowers respondents to furnish 
additional information or elucidate their perspectives. This can be especially beneficial 
in soliciting feedback on products or services, as it permits respondents to articulate 
negative opinions or express concerns. For instance, individuals may refrain from 
endorsing a product due to perceived deficiencies in quality or usability. Allowing them 
to elaborate on their rationale enables organisations to glean valuable insights into 
consumer perceptions and refine their offerings accordingly.
Appropriate Use of “Don’t Know” Responses
While “don’t know” responses may be suitable in surveys tackling sensitive topics, 
their deployment necessitates discretion. In surveys centred on brands or products, it’s 
imperative to ensure respondents possess adequate familiarity with the subject matter 
before soliciting their opinions. Failure to do so may compel respondents to furnish 
ratings for items they have no experience with, potentially distorting the data.
Balancing Response Options for Optimal Survey Design:While “don’t know” 
responses can fulfil a purpose in specific survey configurations, it’s imperative to 
evaluate their impact on data quality and respondent engagement. Incorporating 
alternative response avenues, such as the “other” option, holds the potential to yield 
valuable insights and elevate the effectiveness of surveys.

3.2.8: Missing Data
In the realm of data analysis, the presence of missing data poses significant 
challenges, impacting the accuracy and reliability of research findings. Let’s explore the 
various types of missing data and their implications, along with strategies to address 
them effectively.
Categories of Missing Data
● 
Missing Completely at Random (MCAR): In MCAR scenarios, missing data occur 
randomly across variables, unrelated to any other factors. For example, incomplete 
survey responses due to random dropouts exemplify MCAR data.
● 
Missing at Random (MAR): Despite its name, MAR data display a systematic pattern, 
albeit explainable by observed variables. For instance, in a holiday spending survey, 
missing values may be more common among certain age groups due to privacy 
concerns rather than spending amounts themselves.
● 
Missing Not at Random (MNAR): MNAR data arise due to reasons inherent to the 
values themselves. For instance, individuals with lower incomes may intentionally 
omit reporting holiday spending amounts, thereby skewing the dataset.
Implications of Missing Data
● 
The presence of missing data introduces biases that can compromise the 
representativeness and generalizability of study findings. While MCAR and MAR 
data may be considered ignorable, MNAR data introduce non-ignorable biases that 
can potentially distort conclusions.
Strategies to Handle Missing Data
● 
Preventive Measures: Minimise missing data by designing user-friendly data 
collection forms, employing data validation techniques, and providing incentives 
to participants. Additionally, limiting the number of follow-ups and the volume of 
collected data can help mitigate risks associated with missing data.
● 
Secure Storage: Store collected data securely with multiple backups to mitigate the 
risk of data loss and ensure data integrity.
By comprehensively understanding the nuances of missing data and implementing 
proactive strategies, researchers can effectively address missing data challenges, 
thereby enhancing the reliability and robustness of their analyses

Glossary
● 
Factorial Experimental Design: Factorial design allows the researcher to 
simultaneously examine several factors. It aids in determining the impact of individual 
factors and measuring the combined effect of several variables.
● 
Causal Attributions: A crucial component of an impact evaluation is not just to 
quantify or characterise the changes that have happened, but also to ascertain the 
involvement of certain interventions (such as programmes or policies) in causing 
these changes.
● 
Sole causal attribution: In this sense, the programme or policy is seen to be both 
required and sufficient to generate the desired effects, regardless of external 
variables or other actions.
● 
Alternative (or multiple) causal paths: Here, the plan or policy is only one of numerous 
potential methods to accomplish the desired effects. A specific programme or policy 
can generate the effects, but they can also arise from alternative actions and/or 
external variables.
● 
Statistical Control: Statistical control mostly occurs through the use of several 
control charts, which give essential information that acts as a foundation for taking 
appropriate actions. 
● 
Validity: Validity pertains to the degree of accuracy with which a technique assesses 
the desired measurement.
● 
Reliability: Reliability is a measure of the stability or consistency of every test. We 
can also think of it as the ability for a test or research findings to be repeatable.
