Introduction
Sampling is a fundamental concept in research methodology that involves 
selecting a subset of individuals, items, or data points from a larger population to make 
inferences about that population. This process allows researchers to draw conclusions 
without having to study the entire population, which is often impractical or impossible.
Purpose of Sampling
The main purpose of sampling is to gather data in a way that is manageable and 
cost-effective while still allowing for accurate and reliable conclusions. By studying a 
representative sample, researchers can make generalizations about the broader 
population.
Types of Sampling Methods
1. 
Probability Sampling: Every member of the population has a known, non-zero 
chance of being selected. This method includes techniques such as:


Simple Random Sampling: Every member has an equal chance of being 
selected.


Stratified Sampling: The population is divided into subgroups (strata) based on 
specific characteristics, and samples are drawn from each stratum.


Systematic Sampling: Selecting every nth member from a list of the 
population.


Cluster Sampling: Dividing the population into clusters and randomly selecting 
entire clusters.
2. 
Non-Probability Sampling: Not every member has a known chance of being selected. 
This method includes techniques such as:


Convenience Sampling: Selecting individuals who are easily accessible.


Judgmental or Purposive Sampling: Selecting individuals based on the 
researcher’s judgment about who would be most beneficial to the study.


Quota Sampling: Ensuring certain characteristics are represented in the 
sample to the same proportion as in the population.


Snowball Sampling: Existing study subjects recruit future subjects from among 
their acquaintances.

The size of the sample is critical to the accuracy and reliability of the research 
findings. A larger sample size generally leads to more accurate results, reducing sampling

Reliability of Data:
This issue concerns whether research findings can be applied to a larger group 
than those who participated in the study. To put it another way, would similar results 
have been obtained if a different group of respondents or a different set of data points 
had been used? Is the information obtained from these 40 people sufficient to conclude 
how the entire sales force feels about company policies, for example, if 40 salespeople 
out of a 2,000-person corporate sales force participate in a research study focusing 
on company policy? Would the results be the same if the study was repeated with 40 
different salespeople?
The main goal of reliability is to ensure that the data collection method produces 
consistent results. This can be measured in some types of research by having different 
researchers use the same methods to see if the results can be replicated. If the results 
are similar, the data collection method is most likely reliable. The scientific research 
method includes ensuring that research can be replicated and produces similar results.

Internal validity refers to the extent to which a study can establish a causal relationship

rules out alternative explanations for the observed relationships.

2.2.2	 Types of Questions

2.3.1: Sampling Plan 
Definition of Sampling Plan
The phrase “sampling plan” describes the framework that research projects rely on 
to carry out their investigations. Included are instructions for selecting a representative 
sample from the population, the appropriate sample size, and the category to question.
The research begins with the sampling plan, which encompasses the following 
important decisions:
i. 	
Choose the population
	
The primary and initial step in developing a sample strategy is deciding which subset 
of the target population will be polled.
ii. 	
Determine the Sample Size
 
Decide how many items will be surveyed as part of the sample (the second option in 
the sampling strategy). Since “the larger the sample size, the more is the reliability” 
in general, researchers endeavour to cover a wide number of samples.
iii.	
Decide the Sampling Procedure
	
Choosing the sampling technique, or the approach that may be employed to ensure 
that every object in the population has an equal chance of being picked, is the last 
option that completes the sampling strategy. In most cases, researchers will employ 
probability sampling to pick items that are good representations of the sample.
2.3.2: Sampling Frame
Definition of Sampling Frame
A sampling frame is a list or database from which a sample can be used. In market 
research terms, a sampling frame is a database of potential respondents that can be 
drawn from, to invite to take part in a given research project.

2.3.3: Sample Selection Methods
The sample selection methods can be broadly classified into:
●
●
Probability Sampling
●
●
Non-Probability Sampling
Methods of sampling that use the use of probability to choose individuals or items 
from a population.
There are several probability sampling methods available for examination and 
consideration. These are some of the most renowned choices.
1. 	 Simple random sampling
	
Simple random sampling ensures that each element in the population has an 
equitable probability of being chosen as a component of the sample. It is like 
randomly selecting a name from a hat. Anonymizing the population by assigning a 
unique number to each object or person and then randomly selecting numbers is a 
method for conducting simple random sampling.
 
Conducting simple random sampling is a straightforward and cost-effective method 
that completely eliminates any potential bias in the sample process. Nevertheless, 
it lacks researcher control and may result in the selection of unrepresentative 
categories by chance.
2.	
Systematic sampling
	
Systematic sampling, often referred to as systematic clustering, involves randomly 
selecting only the first item, while the subsequent items are picked in a predetermined 
pattern. Subsequently, a rule is implemented to choose every nth item or person 
thereafter.
	
Despite the presence of randomness, the researcher has the ability to set the interval 
at which items are chosen, ensuring that the selections are not unintentionally 
grouped together.
3.  Stratified sampling
 
Stratified sampling is a method that entails the random selection of individuals from 
pre-established groups. Researchers find it advantageous to possess knowledge 
about the target population, enabling them to strategically divide it (stratify) in a 
manner that is logical and relevant to the research.
 
For instance, when studying travel patterns within a population, it would be beneficial 
to distinguish between those who possess or have access to a car and those who 
rely on public transportation.

Stratified sampling offers advantages, but it also raises the issue of how to divide a 
population into strata, increasing the potential for bias.
4. 	 Cluster sampling
	
Cluster sampling involves randomly selecting groupings, rather than individual 
units, from the target population. These groupings might potentially consist of pre-
established entities, such as individuals residing in specific postal codes or students 
affiliated with a particular academic year.
	
Cluster sampling can be conducted by either picking the entire cluster or, in the 
case of two-stage cluster sampling, randomly selecting the cluster itself and then 
randomly selecting inside the cluster.
Non-probability Sampling Methods
Non-probability sampling methods do not have the same advantages in eliminating 
bias as probability sampling methods. However, there are situations where non-
probability sampling methods are used for their convenience or simplicity. Below are 
some examples of non-probability sampling methods and their respective mechanisms.
1.  Convenience sampling refers to a non-probability sampling technique where 
individuals are selected based on their easy accessibility and convenience.
	
Individuals or components within a sample are chosen based on their accessibility. If 
you are doing a research survey at a university, a convenience sample may include 
students or co-workers who are present on campus and have spare time, and are 
willing to participate in your questionnaire.
	
This type of sample can be valuable, particularly when used as an initial or preliminary 
measure, but it will inevitably introduce considerable bias.
2. 	 Quota sampling is a method of selecting a sample that ensures representation of 
different subgroups in proportion to their presence in the population.
 
Similar to the probability-based stratified sampling method, this strategy seeks 
to ensure representation throughout the target population by selecting survey 
participants based on certain groups or criteria. For instance, your quota may 
encompass a specific count of males and females, or individuals belonging to 
particular age ranges or ethnicities.
	
During the selection process, bias might arise, such as volunteer bias, which may 
lead to a sample that is skewed towards those having enough free time and a specific 
interest in participating. It is possible that bias is inherent in the process of selecting 
categories for quotas by researchers.
3. 	 Purposive sampling is a method of selecting individuals or elements for a study 
based on specific criteria or purpose.
	
Researchers deliberately select participants for the sample based on their expertise 
and comprehension of the study subject or their objectives. Also referred to as 
judgement sampling, this method is unlikely to yield a sample that accurately 
represents the entire population. However, it is a rapid and relatively simple approach 
to obtain a variety of outcomes or replies.
4. 	 Snowball or referral sampling refers to a method of selecting participants for a study 
where existing participants are asked to recommend or refer to other potential 
participants.

2.3.6: Sampling and Non-Sampling Errors
Definition of Sampling Error
Sampling error pertains to discrepancies between the sample and the population 
only due to the particular observations chosen for the sample.
Expanding the sample size will diminish this particular form of mistake.
Definition of Non-Sampling Error:
Non-sampling errors are more serious, and they occur as a result of data 
acquisition errors or incorrect sample observation selection.
Non-Sample Errors
Non-sample errors can be classified into:
●
●
Non-response Error
●
●
Response Error
Non-Response Error
A non-response error arises when the units chosen as part of the sampling process 
fail to provide a complete or partial answer.
Response Error
A response or data error refers to any consistent bias that arises throughout the 
process of data collection, analysis, or interpretation.

data. Surveys and focus group discussions, for example.
● 
Secondary data, on the other hand, is information that has already been gathered 
by someone else. For instance, internet research, newspaper articles, and company 
reports.
●
