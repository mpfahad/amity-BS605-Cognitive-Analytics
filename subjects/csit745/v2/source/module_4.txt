Introduction
Hypothesis testing is a systematic approach to evaluating claims or ideas about 
a group or population. Also known as significance testing, it involves assessing a 
hypothesis or claim about a parameter within a population using data collected from a 
sample. This method helps determine the probability of selecting a sample statistic if 
the hypothesis regarding the population parameter were true.
4.1: Hypothesis Testing
Essentially, hypothesis testing is a statistical technique used to make decisions 
based on experimental data. It revolves around an assumption made about the 
population parameter, which is then tested against observed sample data.
During hypothesis testing, two mutually exclusive statements about a population 
are compared to ascertain which statement is better supported by the sample 
data. When a finding is deemed statistically significant, it indicates strong evidence 
supporting one of the hypotheses tested.
4.1.1 Introduction to Hypothesis
What is a Hypothesis
A hypothesis is a statement about the research problem and may or may not be 
true. It is a tentative and testable relationship between at least two variables that require 
research methodology for testing its authenticity. You are required to test the hypothesis 
and ascertain the truth in the way of research. Theory and hypothesis are completely 
different. Unlike a hypothesis, a theory is a tested, well-substantiated, complete 
explanation for a set of proven factors. 
The term “hypo” means “subject to verification”, and “thesis” means “statement 
about the solution of a problem”. Thus, “hypothesis” is “ a tentative statement, which is 
subject to verification and states the solution of a problem”. Hypothesis offers a solution 
to the research problem that must be verified empirically
Study the below illustration to grasp the concept better.

Testing Hypothesis
The test statistic is a mathematical function or estimator that is calculated using

The critical region, also known as the rejection region, encompasses all values of 
the test statistic that lead to the rejection of the null hypothesis.

rejection region. 
The significance level (represented by α) denotes the probability of the test statistic 
falling within the critical region when the null hypothesis is true.

II)

It’s essential to understand that failing to reject the null hypothesis doesn’t imply

A Type I error transpires when the null hypothesis is rejected despite being true. 
Denoted by α, it represents the probability of this error occurring—specifically, the 
likelihood of rejecting the null hypothesis when it’s actually true.
On the other hand, a Type II error arises when the null hypothesis is not rejected 
even though it’s false. This error is characterised by the probability of failing to reject the 
null hypothesis when it’s actually false, typically denoted by β.

Properties of Estimators
Estimation is a branch of statistics and signal processing that uses measured 
and observed data to determine the values of parameters. Estimation is performed to 
quantify and diagnose the accurate value of a function or a specific piece of information. 
It is conducted by making observations on samples, which are representative subsets 
of the target population or function. Various statistical measures are employed to carry 
out the process of estimation.
Two crucial aspects of estimators that are commonly utilised are the estimator itself 
and the estimate it produces. In order to have a comprehensive understanding of the 
idea of the estimator and estimate, we shall illustrate it using an example. Suppose 
we have a set of samples, denoted as a1, a2, a3, and so on, which are taken from a 
certain population with ‘x’ as its parameter. If T = T(a) is a statistic, then the expected 
value of T(a) is equal to x. These equations demonstrate that a statistical estimation has 
been performed, with the statistic T serving as the estimator and the parameter ‘x’ being 
estimated.
Estimation possesses several crucial attributes for the optimal estimator. These 
attributes encompass characteristics such as impartiality, effectiveness, uniformity, and 
adequacy.
Unbiased estimators are those that yield findings with zero bias for all parameter 
values during estimate. Unbiased estimators are characterised by having the 
mathematical expectation or mean equal to the parameter of the target inquiry. In the 
above example of estimating, T will be considered an unbiased estimator only if its 
estimate is equal to ‘x’.
Consistent estimators are estimators that provide estimates that are reliable and 
accurate. As the number of random variables increases, the degree of concentration 
around the estimate should also grow in order to ensure that the estimator of estimation 
is a consistent estimator. A consistent estimator is one that provides an unbiased 
estimate and has a variance of zero. These two requirements must be satisfied only 
when the number of random variables approaches infinity.
estimating involves many estimators that frequently exhibit consistent behaviour. 
According to the principle of efficiency in estimating, these consistent estimators should 
follow a normal distribution. This feature was considered in the theory of estimating

4.2.11 Model Adequacy Checking
The estimation of parameters and testing of hypotheses about the qualities of the 
estimator are based on certain assumptions, such as: 
1. 	 The relationship between the research variable and explanatory factors is primarily 
linear, with a minimum value occurring around about.
2. 	 The mistake has a mean value of zero. 
3. 	 The mistake exhibits a consistent variance. 
4. 	 The mistakes are not connected. 
5. 	 The mistakes follow a normal distribution.
The accuracy of such assumptions is necessary for the results to have 
significance. If these assumptions are not upheld, the outcome may be inaccurate. 
However, if these deviations are minor, the final result may remain unaffected. However, 
if the deviations are significant, the model derived may become unstable, meaning that 
a new sample may result in a completely different model with contradictory findings. It 
is important to note that these assumptions are based on the population and we are 
just working with a sample. Hence, the primary concern is to establish a selection of 
the population based on a sample of information. Various diagnostic approaches for 
assessing violations of regression assumptions involve analysing model residuals using 
a variety of graphical techniques.

Glossary
● 
Hypothesis: A hypothesis is a statement about the research problem and may or 
may not be true. It is a tentative and testable relationship between at least two 
variables that require research methodology for testing its authenticity. 
● 
Simple Hypothesis: In a simple hypothesis, there exists a relationship between 
two variables; one is called an independent variable or cause, and the other is a 
dependent variable or effect.
● 
Complex Hypothesis: In a complex hypothesis, there exists a relationship among 
more variables (more than two dependent and independent).
● 
Empirical / Working Hypothesis: Working hypothesis is mainly applied to a particular 
field. At the time of formulation, it is an assumption, but when it is ready for a test to 
become an empirical or working hypothesis. 
● 
Logical Hypothesis: Here, the hypothesis is rationally validated. J.S. Mill has proposed 
four principles of this theory, including agreement, disagreement, difference, and 
residue.
● 
Statistical Hypothesis: A hypothesis may be logical or illogical but can be verified 
statistically, called a statistical hypothesis.
