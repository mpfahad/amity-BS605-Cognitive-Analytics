Introduction
Authors and management experts have defined research in various ways. 
Typically, research is said to commence with a question or a problem. It is described 
as the creation of new concepts, methodologies, and understandings through the 
development of new knowledge or the innovative application of existing knowledge. 
This process often involves synthesizing and analysing previous studies to produce 
new and groundbreaking results.
1.1 Research
Through research, we can identify solutions to problems by employing systematic 
and scientific methods. This may involve experimentation or innovation, with “risk” 
highlighting the inherent dangers of discovery. 
1.1.1 Definition and Objectives of Research
What is research?
Investigation might lead to analysis, and tests could be conducted to verify 
hypotheses. This work is distinct and unprecedented, offering new insights as a 
result. It could introduce a novel method or new data source, leading to significant 
breakthroughs or incremental improvements in current practices. Research is essential 
for progress, as it is fundamentally tied to “research and development.”
Slesinger & Stephenson, Encyclopaedia of Social Sciences:
“the manipulation of things, concepts or symbols for the purpose of generalizing to 
extend, correct or verify knowledge, whether that knowledge aids in the construction of 
theory or in the practice of an art”.
●
●
Redman & Mory: “Systematized effort to gain new knowledge”. It is an academic 
activity and should be used in a technical sense.
●
●
Clifford Woody: Research comprises “defining and redefining problems, 
formulating hypotheses or suggested solutions; collecting, organizing and 
evaluating data; making deductions and reaching conclusions; and finally, carefully 
testing the conclusions to determine whether they fit the formulated hypotheses”.

1.1.3 Differences b/w Research Methods and Research Methodology
Although the names sound similar, both Research methods and Research 
methodology are different, as explained below:
Research Methods
The various procedures, schemes, steps, and algorithms used in research are 
known as research methods. The term “research methods” refers to all of the methods 
used by a researcher during a research study. They’re primarily planned, scientific, and 
value-agnostic. Observations, theoretical procedures, experimental studies, numerical 
schemes, statistical approaches, and so on are all examples of these. We can use 
research methods to collect samples, data, and come up with a solution to a problem. 
Business and scientific research methods, in particular, demand explanations based on 
collected facts, measurements, and observations, rather than solely on reasoning. They 
only accept explanations that can be verified through experiments.
Research Methodology
A systematic approach to solving a problem is known as research methodology. It 
is a science that studies how research should be conducted. Research methodology 
is essentially the procedures by which researchers go about their work of describing, 
explaining, and predicting phenomena. It can also be defined as the study of methods 
for gaining knowledge. Its goal is to provide a research work plan.
1.2 Research Process
In the early decades human inquiry was primarily based on the examination of 
one’s own conscious thoughts and feelings that means the observation of any one and 
understanding through logical discussion to seek the truth. This process served as a 
well-established conceptual framework for comprehending the world and was accepted 
for a century. An essential component of the investigation process was the knowledge 
seeker. With time, this part was changed. The Scientific method introduced several 
major components in research procedures like Objectivity.

1.2.2: Approaches of Research: Deductive, Inductive, Quantitative 
and Qualitative
Deductive: Deductive approach starts with developing a hypothesis based on 
existing theory, and then prepares a research strategy for testing the hypothesis. To 
reason deductively is to go from the specific to the general. In many circumstances, 
a theory or case study that suggests a causal relationship or correlation may be 
accurate. The term “deductive approach” refers to a method of formulating hypotheses 
or drawing conclusions from theoretical ideas. The deductive method is concerned with 
drawing conclusions from statements or premises. By “testing against observations,” 
deductive reasoning begins with a pattern, whereas induction methodology “begins with 
observations and seeks to find a pattern within them.”

1.2.5: Errors in Research
Various types of errors in Research are described below.
1. 	 Population Specification
Population specification errors occur when the researcher does not understand 
whom they should survey. Because there are more than one people who may consume 
the product, but only one who purchases it, or they may miss a segment looking to 
purchase in the future.
How to avoid this: In the beginning analysis of customers buying the items have to 
be done. This is necessary as it will allow us to identify the appropriate customers and 
ways to satisfy them.
2. 	 Sampling and Sample Frame Errors
When the wrong subpopulation is used to select a sample then survey 
sampling and sample frame errors occur, or because of variation in the number or 
representativeness( is a subset of a population that seeks to accurately reflect the 
characteristics of the larger group) of the sample that responds, but the resulting 
sample is not representative of the population concern.
Basically some element of sampling error is unavoidable, but it can be predictable. 
For instance, in the 1936 presidential election between Roosevelt and Landon, the 
sample frame was from car registrations and telephone directories. The researchers 
failed to realize that the majority of people that owned cars and telephones were 
Republicans, and wrongly predicted a Republican victory.
Example: Assume that we obtained a random sample of 5000 individuals from 
the whole adult population of India in order to determine their preferences about 
entertainment. Upon examination, it was determined that the composition of the group 
consisted of 70% females. The entertainment tastes of females would have more 
significance, making it difficult to accurately generalise to the overall adult population 
in India. The homogeneity of a sample has an impact on the sampling error( the quality 
of consisting of parts or people that are similar to each other) of the population being 
studied and sampled from and by the size of the sample.
How to avoid this: Since it cannot be avoided completely, then we should have 
multiple people reviewing our sample to account for an accurate representation of 
our target population. We can also increase the size of our sample so we get more 
participants to survey.
3. 	 Selection

occur. This error arises when respondents choose to self-participate in a study and only

4. 	 Non-responsive
Non-response errors occur when there is a discrepancy between the original 
sample and the selected sample. This discrepancy arises when potential respondents 
are either not contacted or refuse to respond. The primary issue here is the absence 
of data rather than inaccurate data. Non-response errors can be categorized into two 
types:
(a)  Non-contact errors: These occur when the respondent cannot be reached, possibly 
because they are not at home.
(b)  Refusal errors: These occur when the respondent chooses not to respond to specific 
items or multiple items in the questionnaire. Topics such as monthly household 
income, religion, sex, and politics are more likely to result in item refusal.
Example: In the event of conducting telephone surveys, certain respondents 
may be deemed inaccessible due to their absence from home during the first call or 
subsequent call-backs. The remaining individuals have either relocated or are now 
absent from their residences during the survey period. Respondents who are not at 
home are usually younger and do not have young children. 
Additionally, a larger percentage of these families have women who are employed 
compared to households where someone is at home. Individuals who have relocated or 
are now absent throughout the survey period exhibit a greater degree of geographical 
mobility compared to the general population. The majority of surveys may predict 
inaccuracies resulting from the lack of touch with respondents. Online surveys can 
prevent this problem by distributing them over email, therefore excluding respondents 
who are not at home.
How can one prevent or steer clear of this situation? When gathering replies, 
it is important to ensure that our initial respondents are actively engaged. If they do 
not respond initially, we should employ follow-up surveys and alternative methods of 
reaching out to them. Additionally, we may employ several channels, such as face-
to-face interactions, web surveys, or SMS, to effectively communicate with our target 
audience.

Cross-sectional study
The longitudinal and cross-sectional studies are both observational studies. This 
means that researchers record data about their subjects without tampering with the 
research environment. We would simply measure the cholesterol levels of daily walkers 
and non-walkers, as well as any other.
Characteristics of interest to us, in our study. We would not persuade non-walkers

A cross-sectional study is distinguished by the ability to compare different 
population groups at a single point in time. Consider it like taking a photograph. The 
findings are based on whatever fits into the frame.
To return to our previous example, we could compare cholesterol levels in daily 
walkers in two age groups, over 40 and under 40, to cholesterol levels in non-walkers 
in the same age groups. We might even create gender subgroups. We would not, 
however, consider past or future cholesterol levels because they would be outside the 
scope. We’d only examine cholesterol levels at a single point in time.
A cross-sectional study design has the advantage of allowing researchers to 
compare multiple variables at once. For instance, with minimal extrapolation, we may 
examine the correlation between walking and cholesterol levels and demographic 
variables such as age, gender, income, and education level.
Cross-sectional studies, on the other hand, may not provide conclusive evidence 
of cause-and-effect relationships. This is because such studies provide a snapshot of 
a single moment in time and do not consider what occurs before or after the snapshot. 
As a result, we can’t say for sure whether our daily walkers had low cholesterol levels 
before starting their exercise routines or if the daily walking behaviour helped to lower 
cholesterol levels that were previously high.

A longitudinal study has the advantage of allowing researchers to detect changes 
in the characteristics of the target population at both the group and individual level. The 
important thing to remember is that longitudinal studies go beyond a single point in 
time. As a result, they can create event sequences.

1.4.2: Model Building and Stages
  Model building is a crucial aspect of research methodology, especially in fields 
such as data science, engineering, and social sciences. It involves creating abstract 
representations of real-world systems or phenomena to understand, analyze, and 
predict their behavior. The process of model building in research follows several 
essential stages to develop a reliable and valid model.
Firstly, the problem must be defined clearly by identifying the objectives and 
understanding the scope, objectives, and constraints of the study. This includes 
gathering relevant data and information to understand the context and requirements of 
the model.
Next, conceptualization involves developing a theoretical framework based 
on existing literature and theories related to the problem. This helps in formulating 
hypotheses and identifying key variables. A preliminary model is then created to outline 
the relationships between different variables and components, often represented 
diagrammatically.
The data collection stage involves identifying and gathering data from primary 
and secondary sources such as surveys, experiments, observations, and databases. 
Ensuring data quality is crucial, as the data must be accurate, reliable, and relevant to 
the research problem, often requiring data cleaning and preprocessing.
In the model specification stage, the conceptual model is translated into a 
mathematical or statistical form. This involves selecting appropriate modeling 
techniques and specifying equations or algorithms. Any assumptions made during this 
stage must be clearly stated, as they can impact the model’s validity.
Model estimation involves using statistical or computational methods to estimate 
the parameters of the model, employing techniques such as regression analysis or 
machine learning algorithms. The model parameters are then calibrated to improve 
accuracy and fit to the observed data.
Validation of the model is crucial to ensure it is correctly implemented and free from 
logical or computational errors. The model’s predictions are compared with real-world 
data to assess accuracy and reliability, which may involve cross-validation, sensitivity 
analysis, and robustness checks.
Once validated, the model is implemented to analyze the research problem, make 
predictions, or provide insights. This stage involves using the model for decision-making 
or further research. Optimization of the model is also necessary based on feedback and 
results, potentially iterating through previous stages to enhance performance.
Finally, the results are interpreted in the context of the research problem, 
understanding the implications and limitations. The entire modeling process, including 
the methodology, assumptions, data sources, and findings, is documented to ensure 
transparency and reproducibility. The results are then reported clearly and concisely 
through research papers, reports, or presentations, including visualizations and 
explanations of the model’s implications.

Shelly Chaiken developed the heuristic-systematic model of information processing 
(HSM), which attempts to explain how people receive and process persuasive 
messages. 1st Individuals can process messages in one of two ways, according to the 
model: heuristically or systematically. Heuristic processing, on the other hand, entails

Simulation Research Modelling
By using statistical descriptions of the activities involved, stimulation models 
attempt to replicate the workings and logic of a real system. For example, a line 
might produce 1000 units per hour on average. If we assume this is always the case, 
we lose sight of what happens when there is a breakdown or a stoppage for routine 
maintenance, for example. When we consider the effect on downstream units, the effect 
of such a delay may be amplified (or absorbed).
‘Entities’ (e.g. machines, materials, people, etc.) and ‘activities’ are two types of 
entities in a simulation model (e.g. processing, transporting, etc.). It also includes an 
explanation of the logic that governs each activity. A processing activity, for example, 
can only begin when a certain quantity of working material, a person to operate the 
machine, and an empty conveyor to transport the product are all available. Once an 
activity has begun, the time it will take to complete it is calculated, which is frequently 
done using a sample from a statistical distribution.
